Most learners notice it eventually. You say something in Japanese you would
never say in your first language. You ask the question. You approach the
stranger. You are, briefly, a slightly braver person.

This is documented. It has a name: 外国語効果, the foreign language effect,
and about fifteen years of research behind it.

## The finding

The anchor study is Keysar, Hayakawa and An (2012), in *Psychological
Science*. They gave people the same decisions in their native language and in
a foreign one. In the foreign language, participants were measurably less
swayed by how the choice was framed, and more willing to take a bet with good
odds.

Costa and colleagues extended it to moral dilemmas in 2014: people were more
likely to choose the coldly utilitarian option when the problem was posed in a
second language.

The effect has replicated across many language pairs. Note the caveat
early, though: it is a **statistical tendency across groups**, not a switch
inside you. Later meta-analyses find the moral-dilemma version weaker than the
first papers suggested.

## Three mechanisms

### 1. 心理的距離 · psychological distance

Your 母語 was learned alongside the events that gave it weight. The word for
*shame* arrived attached to an actual afternoon of being ashamed. Words learned
later, in classrooms and apps, arrive without that freight.

Caldwell-Harris has shown this physically: taboo words and reprimands in a
first language produce a stronger skin-conductance response than the same
words in a second. The second-language version is genuinely quieter in the
body.

This is why some people find *I love you* easier in a language that is not
theirs. The sentence does not cost what it costs at home.

### 2. More deliberate processing

A second language will not run on autopilot. It demands conscious effort,
which widens the gap between impulse and speech.

That gap is usually described as a cost. For confidence it behaves like a
feature. The reflexive, self-monitoring, pre-apologising phrasing that a
native speaker produces without noticing simply does not have time to form.

### 3. Separation from the 社会的自己

Your first language is bound to a social role: the version of you your family
recognises, the register you use with colleagues who have known you a decade.

Japanese was not present when those roles were built. Speaking it puts you
slightly outside the person you are expected to be, which is uncomfortable in
some settings and liberating in exactly the ones where you would normally
hesitate.

## The fourth reason, which the research does not cover

Here is the honest gap. **The studies above are about decision-making, not
about confidence in conversation.** They measure framing bias and moral
choices, not whether you feel brave enough to talk to someone at a bar.

The bridge between the two is plausible and unproven. And the mechanism you
feel most directly may be the simplest one:

> You can afford to make mistakes, because it is your second language.

A learner is granted a lower standard, and knows it. Errors are read as
learning rather than as carelessness or stupidity. Nobody updates their
opinion of your intelligence when you conjugate something wrong.

That is not psychological distance. It is **reduced social accountability**,
and for speaking specifically, it is probably doing more work than any of the
three mechanisms above.

## What this means for practice

If the ease comes partly from lowered stakes, then the way to keep it is to
keep the stakes low on purpose. Speak before you are ready. Treat the mistake
as the price of the sentence rather than evidence about you.

The confidence is real. It is just borrowed from the fact that you are allowed
to be bad at this, so use it while the loan is open.
